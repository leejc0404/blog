Pink Seat Subway Rules: The Real Reason You Can't Sit There in Korea

Last updated: July 2026  |  7 min read

It happens in the first week: you drop into an empty pink seat on a packed Line 2 train after a long day of sightseeing. Within thirty seconds, three different passengers are staring like a law has been broken. No law has been broken, technically — but one of Korea's most quietly enforced subway rules has. If you have ever wondered why a handful of Seoul Metro seats are painted bright pink, and why locals treat them so differently from the regular priority seats, you are about to learn the pink seat subway rules that every foreigner eventually learns the hard way, usually while a train full of strangers watches. The short answer: pink seats in the Seoul Metro are reserved exclusively for pregnant women at any stage of pregnancy — not for the elderly, the disabled, or tired commuters, no matter how empty the seat looks. Even most long-term residents avoid them entirely. Here is exactly why, and what you should do instead.

What Are Korea's Pink Subway Seats, Exactly?

Seoul's subway has run general priority seating since 1985, according to Seoul Metro's official history, but by 2013 there was a specific problem: pregnant women, especially in early pregnancy when nothing shows yet, kept getting harassed or ignored when they tried to sit in the regular priority seats. The city's answer — launched in 2013 as part of a Seoul Metropolitan Government maternal welfare initiative — was the “Pink Light” campaign — a set of seats near the doors in every subway car, painted bright pink, marked with a pregnant-woman icon, and reserved only for expectant mothers. They are not general priority seats. They are not for the elderly, for people with injuries, or for anyone carrying heavy bags. They exist for one purpose, and Seoul wants that purpose to be unmistakable from across a crowded car. The pink seat subway rules were also a response to something Korean transit officials openly admitted: people simply were not giving up regular seats for pregnant women who did not visibly look pregnant. A dedicated pink seat sidesteps the whole judgment call. If the seat is pink, you do not sit there. It does not matter how tired you are, how crowded the train is, or how empty the seat looks.

Who Is Actually Allowed to Sit There?

Any pregnant woman, at any stage of pregnancy, is entitled to a pink seat — including women in their first trimester who show no visible signs. Many carry a small pink keychain badge issued by the city, which they clip to a bag or stroller to signal, discreetly, that they qualify. Some Busan transit lines have gone a step further with a wireless beacon system: when a badge-holder approaches, a small pink light near the seat activates and chimes, prompting anyone sitting there to stand up. As a tourist or expat, the practical rule is simple: treat every pink seat as occupied, even when it is empty. Korean commuters do this reflexively, often standing through an entire ride rather than sitting in a pink seat just for one stop. Adopting the same habit is the fastest way to avoid an awkward, very public correction.

Pink Seats vs. the Silver Priority Seats

This is where most visitors get confused, because Seoul's subway actually runs two separate seating systems side by side. The general priority seats — sometimes called the silver seats, 닗약자석 — sit at the very ends of each car and are meant for the elderly, people with disabilities, and pregnant women alike. The pink seats are a smaller, separate carve-out specifically for pregnant women, usually located near the doors in the middle of the car rather than at the very ends. In practice, this means a pregnant rider actually has two seating options, while everyone else only has zero — neither category is open to healthy adults regardless of how tired they are. Why the Pink Seat Rule Causes So Much Tension

This is not a minor cultural quirk — it is an active, ongoing dispute in Korean society. According to The Korea Herald, complaints about non-pregnant passengers occupying pink seats run into the thousands every year. Seoul Metro itself has acknowledged fielding roughly 7,000 complaints annually about the pink seats alone — more than twenty every single day. Part of the tension is invisible-illness logic: someone eight weeks pregnant looks, to a stranger, exactly like someone who is simply choosing to sit in a seat they should not. Koreans have collectively decided to resolve that ambiguity by making the seats untouchable by default, rather than relying on case-by-case judgment. For a foreign visitor unfamiliar with the system, sitting down without knowing the color-coding can look like willful disrespect even when it is pure ignorance.

Where You Will See Pink Seats

Pink seats are standard across Seoul Metro Lines 1 through 9, usually the two seats closest to a set of doors. Busan's subway system runs a similar pink-seat program with beacon technology. According to the official Seoul city travel guide, seat awareness is listed as one of the core public transportation etiquette points for visitors.

What to Actually Do as a Foreign Visitor

Treat pink seats as always occupied, even when visibly empty. Assume any woman near a pink seat with a small pink keychain badge is pregnant, and offer the seat without being asked. If travelling with a visibly pregnant partner, point her toward the pink seat directly. Pink Seat Subway Rules: Quick Answers

Is there an actual fine for sitting in a pink seat in Korea? No. There is no legal penalty — the enforcement is entirely social. You will not be stopped by staff, but you may be stared at, asked to move, or scolded by an older passenger. What if the car is nearly empty and no pregnant woman is in sight? Korean commuters still tend to leave pink seats open even on quiet trains. Following the same habit costs you nothing and avoids any risk of an awkward moment when the car fills back up. Are pink seats only a Seoul thing? No — Busan runs a version of the same pink seat subway rules with its beacon technology. Other regional systems mostly rely on the general priority seating norm without a dedicated pink seat. I am pregnant but not showing yet — how do I actually get the seat? Korea's official pink badge keychains exist for exactly this situation. Foreign residents can usually request one through their local district office or maternity clinic.
